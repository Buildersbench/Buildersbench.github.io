How to Price a Lawn Mowing Job by Lot Size, Frequency, and Extras

November 18, 2026

A homeowner asks what you charge to mow their lawn. You look at the yard, give them a number, and hope you have not missed anything. But two lawns that look similar can take very different amounts of time. Here is how to work out a mowing price that covers your costs and gives the customer a clear quote.

Do not price by lot size alone

Lot size gives you a starting point, but not how long the job will take. A flat, open lawn is different from one with trees, fences, slopes, and beds to work around.

Look at the actual mowing area, not the total property size. A large property with little grass can take less time than a smaller one with complicated landscaping.

Also check access. A narrow gate, a long walk from the trailer, or a backyard that needs smaller equipment can add time. Walk the property or ask for clear photos before committing to a price.

Work out your cost per visit

Start with labor: unloading, mowing, trimming, edging, and blowing clippings away. If you have employees, use their actual cost. If you do the work yourself, put a value on your own time.

Add fuel, equipment wear, maintenance, travel, and overhead. Your mower does not run for free, and your truck costs money on every trip, short or long.

Then decide what profit margin you need. Adding a percentage to your costs is not a profit margin. If you want a 20 percent margin, divide your total cost by 0.80.

A worked example for a regular mowing customer

Say a property takes an hour and fifteen minutes to mow, trim, and clean up. Your labor at 30 dollars an hour is 37.50. Travel costs 15, fuel and equipment 8, and allocated overhead 9.

Your total cost is 69.50 dollars per visit.

For a 20 percent profit margin, divide 69.50 by 0.80. That gives you 86.88 dollars, so a weekly price of 90 covers those costs and leaves a little room for unexpected expenses.

Now consider the same property every other week. The grass takes longer, so if labor rises to 52.50 and fuel and equipment to 11, your total cost becomes 87.50. Divide that by 0.80 and you get 109.38.

That is why every other week customers should not get the weekly price. The work is different.

Charge separately for extra work

A mowing quote should say exactly what the customer gets: edging, trimming around trees and fences, blowing clippings off the driveway and sidewalk, and hauling away grass clippings.

Suppose a customer wants extra edging that takes fifteen minutes. At 30 dollars an hour, that adds 7.50 in labor, plus 1.50 in equipment, so the extra costs you 9 dollars. At the same 20 percent margin, charge 11.25. Quoting 12 keeps it from becoming unpaid labor.

Write these services and prices down, so you can explain the extra charge before doing work outside the regular agreement.
