How to Tell If a Small Repair Job Is Worth Taking

November 19, 2026

Not every handyman job is worth accepting. A customer might call about a loose cabinet door, a broken shelf, or a small drywall repair that sounds easy enough. But once you account for travel, preparation, materials, and the time you could have spent on another job, that small repair might not be as profitable as it looks. Here is how to decide whether a job deserves a place on your schedule.

Look beyond the time spent doing the repair

The first thing to consider is how much time the entire appointment will take. The repair itself might only take thirty minutes, but you also have to account for driving, loading tools, setting up, cleaning up, and handling payment.

For example, suppose a cabinet repair takes thirty minutes. Driving takes twenty minutes each way, and preparation and cleanup take another twenty minutes. You have spent an hour and a half on the appointment, even though the actual repair took only half an hour.

That difference matters when you are deciding whether the customer is offering enough money to make the trip worthwhile.

Calculate the real cost before accepting

Look at the labor, travel, materials, and other expenses associated with the job. Include the cost of consumables, equipment use, and the portion of your regular business overhead that needs to be covered.

Suppose you expect to spend an hour and a half on a repair. Your labor cost is 45 dollars per hour, making the labor cost 67.50 dollars. Fuel and vehicle expenses add 12 dollars, materials cost 8 dollars, and allocated overhead adds 10 dollars.

Your total job cost is 97.50 dollars.

The customer offers 125 dollars. That is 27.50 dollars of profit after the listed costs. The job makes money on paper, but you still need to decide whether that return is worth using the time.

Consider what you are giving up

Your schedule has limited space. Accepting one small repair might mean turning down another appointment that would have earned more.

Suppose you have a two-hour opening. You could use it for the repair above, or you could accept another job with an expected selling price of 220 dollars and estimated costs of 130 dollars.

The small repair produces 27.50 dollars after its estimated costs, while the larger job produces 90 dollars. If both customers are available during the same time slot, the larger job is the better use of your schedule.

However, the small repair might still make sense if it is close to another appointment, fills an otherwise empty period, or creates an opportunity for additional work.

Watch for growing jobs and track what pays

A small repair can become an expensive appointment when the original problem turns out to be more complicated than expected. A loose shelf might reveal damaged wall anchors. A leaking fixture might involve a problem behind the cabinet.

Ask customers for photos and a clear description before accepting the work. Explain what your price covers and what would require an additional charge.

If you discover extra work after arriving, stop and explain the situation. Give the customer an updated price and get approval before continuing.

After completing small repairs, record the actual revenue, labor time, materials, and other expenses. Compare those results with your original estimate.

You may find that certain repairs are consistently profitable while others take too long or require more materials than expected. Use that information when deciding which calls to accept in the future.
