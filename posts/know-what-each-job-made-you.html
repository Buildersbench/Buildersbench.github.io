How to tell if a job actually made you money

November 11, 2026

Revenue is not profit. A job that pays $400 and costs you $380 in materials, labor, and overhead is a $20 job — and if you do four of those a week, you are working full time for $80.

The three costs

Every job has three costs, and the quote has to cover all three or the job loses money quietly.

Materials. What the job consumed: paint, parts, cleaning supplies, sealant. Not what you have sitting in the van — what this job used up.

Labor, at a real rate. Your own hours count, and they count at the rate you would pay someone else to do the work — not at zero. If a handyman job takes you six hours and you value your time at $50 an hour, that is $300 of labor whether or not anyone wrote you a paycheck for it. Pricing your own time at zero is the fastest way to stay busy and broke.

Overhead. Fuel, insurance, your phone, the wear on your tools and your vehicle. No single job carries all of it, but every job carries a share. A simple way to handle it: add up your monthly overhead, divide it by the number of jobs you do in a month, and charge that share to each job.

Profit per hour is the number that matters

Profit per job can lie to you. A $500-profit job that took three days is worse than a $150-profit job that took an afternoon. Divide the profit by the hours the job took, including travel, and compare jobs on that number. The jobs with the best profit per hour are the ones to chase; the ones at the bottom are the ones to reprice or stop taking.

The "done but not paid" trap

A job is not money until it is collected. Done-but-unpaid work is the most common hole in a small service business: the job is finished, the invoice went out, and the money sits in someone else's account for three weeks. Track unpaid balances separately from profit, and check them every week. A polite message — "Just checking in on invoice 1041, due last Friday" — collects more money than waiting does.

Review the month, not just the job

Once a month, look at the whole picture: total revenue, total profit, average margin, your best job, and your worst job. The pattern usually jumps out fast. One trade or one kind of job carries the profit; another kind eats it. That monthly review is what turns a busy schedule into a business that actually pays you.
