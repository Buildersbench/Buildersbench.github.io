How to quote a cleaning job without undercharging

November 11, 2026

Most undercharging happens before you pick up a mop. The quote left out travel, setup, and the hours nobody bills for — and by the time you notice, the job is done and the money is gone.

Walk the job first

Never quote a cleaning job sight unseen if you can help it. A three-bedroom house with two dogs and a toddler is not the same job as a three-bedroom house with one tidy retiree. Walk through it, count the bathrooms, look at the floors, and ask how long it has been since the last deep clean. A first clean is almost always a deep clean, even when the client calls it a regular clean — price it that way.

Price the whole job, not just the hour

An hourly rate feels simple, but the hours on site is only part of what the job costs you. A real quote covers four things:

Time on site. The hours you are actually cleaning.

Travel and setup. Driving there, unloading, and putting everything away afterward. If a job takes two hours to clean and forty minutes round trip, you spent nearly three hours on it.

Supplies. What you use on the job — and if you bring your own equipment, a share of what it costs to replace.

The hours nobody bills for. Quoting, messaging, scheduling, invoicing. If you bill 25 hours a week, your rate has to carry the other 15. This is the part most new cleaners miss, and it is why a $25-an-hour rate can still lose money.

Put it in writing

A written quote protects both sides. It should say what is included, what is extra, when the price is good until, and what the deposit is. "Kitchen, two baths, living room, and three bedrooms, including floors, surfaces, and trash — $145, valid for 14 days" is a quote. "Around $140ish" is a misunderstanding waiting to happen.

Write down the extras too: inside the oven, inside the fridge, windows, laundry. If the client asks for one of those on the day, you can say yes and charge for it — because the quote already says it was not included.

Take a deposit

A deposit does two things. It covers you if the client cancels the morning of, and it tells you the client is serious. Half up front is normal for a first clean. For repeat clients on a regular schedule, a deposit on the first visit and payment on the day after that is a fair pattern.

When the job runs long

It will, sometimes. The rule is simple: flag it before you do the extra work, not after. "This is going to take another hour — that's $45. Do you want me to go ahead?" gets you paid. Doing the extra hour quietly and adding it to the invoice gets you an argument.
