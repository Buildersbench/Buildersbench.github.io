How to Estimate Mulch Installation Jobs and Price Them Profitably

November 19, 2026

Mulch installation looks straightforward until you start buying materials, loading the truck, and spreading everything across the property. A customer sees a finished flower bed, but you have to account for the mulch, delivery, labor, equipment, and time spent preparing the area. If you estimate only the material cost and add a little for labor, you can easily underprice the job. Here is how to calculate what you need and build a price that makes sense.

Measure the beds before ordering mulch

Start by measuring the actual planting beds rather than estimating from a quick look at the property. Measure the length and width of each section, then add the areas together.

You also need to agree on the depth. A customer asking for a light refresh is not necessarily asking for the same amount of material as someone who wants a completely new layer.

To calculate cubic yards, multiply the total square footage by the depth in inches, then divide by 324.

For example, suppose the beds cover 600 square feet and the customer wants three inches of mulch. The calculation is 600 multiplied by three, divided by 324. That gives you approximately 5.56 cubic yards.

You should order enough to complete the job rather than relying on a rounded-down estimate. In this example, ordering six cubic yards gives you a little room for uneven ground and material loss.

Calculate the full cost of the job

Once you know how much mulch you need, get the actual supplier price. Include delivery separately if it is not already included in the material price.

Next, estimate the labor. Include unloading, moving the mulch, spreading it evenly, cleaning up the beds, and clearing the driveway or other areas where material has been dropped.

Equipment also has a cost. Wheelbarrows, shovels, rakes, trucks, and other equipment need maintenance and eventual replacement. Even if you already own everything, the job still uses those resources.

Finally, include travel and your share of business overhead. A nearby job and one across your service area should not automatically carry the same cost.

A worked example for a mulch installation

Suppose you need six cubic yards of mulch at 38 dollars per yard. Materials cost 228 dollars, and delivery costs 75 dollars.

Your crew consists of two people working three and a half hours each at an actual labor cost of 28 dollars per hour. That gives you 196 dollars in labor.

Add 25 dollars for equipment and 35 dollars for allocated overhead. Your total job cost is 559 dollars.

If you want a 20 percent profit margin, divide 559 by 0.80. Your selling price needs to be 698.75 dollars, so you could quote 700 dollars.

That is your starting price for the agreed scope. If the customer also wants old mulch removed, that needs its own labor and disposal calculation.

Keep extra work separate and check the actual profit

One of the easiest ways to lose money on landscaping jobs is to include additional work without charging for it.

Old mulch removal, weed removal, bed edging, landscape fabric, and moving existing plants can all add time. Do not assume these tasks are included just because the customer asked for mulch installation.

Describe exactly what the customer is getting. State the mulch type, approximate depth, areas being covered, and whether cleanup and disposal are included.

If the customer changes the scope after you have started, calculate the additional cost and get approval before doing the extra work.

Your estimate is only useful if you compare it with what happened on the property. Record the actual material cost, crew hours, delivery expense, and equipment costs after finishing the job.

If you estimated a job using a certain amount of labor but the crew needed substantially longer, find out why. Maybe the beds were harder to access, the measurements were wrong, or the original scope was incomplete.

Keep those results for the next estimate. Your own completed jobs are the best way to improve your future pricing.
