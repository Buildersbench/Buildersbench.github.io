How to Estimate Drywall Repair Jobs Including Patching, Texture, and Paint

November 20, 2026

Drywall repair looks simple until you start working through everything the customer expects you to fix. A hole in the wall might need backing, several coats of compound, sanding, texture matching, primer, and paint. If you quote only for patching the hole, you can end up doing much more work than the customer is paying for.

Check the Damage Before Quoting

Start by looking at the size and location of the damage. A small hole from a door handle is different from a damaged section that needs to be cut out and replaced. Ceiling repairs also take more effort than many wall repairs because you are working overhead.

Look for signs of moisture or other damage around the area. If the drywall is soft, stained, or still wet, find out whether the underlying problem has been fixed. Do not promise a finished repair when the source of the damage is still active.

Take photos and record the approximate size of each repair. If the customer has several damaged areas, inspect all of them rather than pricing the job based on the first hole you see.

Separate Patching From Finishing Work

One mistake is treating drywall repair as a single task. Patching, taping, applying compound, sanding, matching texture, priming, and painting all take time.

Decide what the customer actually wants. Some customers only need a patch that is ready for their painter. Others expect you to match the existing wall and leave the repair painted.

Texture matching deserves particular attention. Orange peel, knockdown, and smooth finishes require different techniques. Matching an older wall can take more effort than applying a standard finish to a new patch.

Also consider drying time. A repair that needs multiple coats may require another visit. That means additional travel, setup, and time away from other jobs. Make sure those return visits are included in your estimate.

Calculate Labor and Materials Separately

Work out the materials you need before putting together the price. Include drywall, backing material, tape, joint compound, sandpaper, primer, paint, and any supplies needed to protect the work area.

Then estimate the labor for each part of the job.

For example, suppose you estimate 3 hours for patching and finishing, 1 hour for texture and preparation, and 1 hour for priming and painting. At an internal labor cost of $35 per hour, your labor cost is $175.

If materials cost $45 and travel and other job expenses add another $20, your estimated direct cost is $240.

This is not yet your customer price. You still need to account for business overhead and the profit you expect to earn.

Write a Quote That Explains the Scope

Put each major part of the repair into the written estimate. Show the patching, finishing, texture matching, and painting separately when those tasks are part of the job.

Be clear about what is excluded. If the customer needs a plumber to repair a leak before you can replace the drywall, state that plumbing work is not included. If you are painting only the repaired area, explain that the new paint may not blend perfectly with an older wall.

For the example job, suppose you add $30 for overhead and want $80 in profit. Your quote would be $350 based on the estimated $240 direct cost.

After the job, record the actual time and material expenses. If drywall repairs consistently take longer than expected, adjust your estimates instead of absorbing the difference on every job.

A clear estimate protects both you and the customer. They know what work they are approving, and you know what you need to complete before calling the job finished.
