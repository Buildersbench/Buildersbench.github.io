How to Charge for Pet Hair Removal Without Underpricing Interior Details

November 20, 2026

Pet hair removal is one of those detailing jobs that can take much longer than customers expect. A quick vacuum might handle a few loose hairs, but embedded hair in carpets, seats, and cargo areas can turn a normal interior detail into a much longer job. If you include all pet hair removal in your regular interior package, you can lose money on the vehicles that need the most work.

Check the Amount of Pet Hair Before Quoting

Do not assume every vehicle with a dog or cat needs the same amount of extra work. Some customers have a little hair on the seats, while others have hair packed deep into carpet fibers and around seat tracks.

Ask customers to send photos before you give a final price. Look at the seats, floor mats, carpet, trunk, and areas around the center console. Pay attention to how deeply the hair is embedded, not just how much you can see on the surface.

You should also ask whether the customer wants a standard interior cleaning or a more thorough pet hair removal service. Make sure both sides understand what is included before you book the job.

Set Different Prices for Different Conditions

A flat pet hair fee can work for vehicles that have roughly the same amount of work. But if one vehicle takes a few extra minutes and another takes a full additional hour, charging the same amount does not make sense.

Consider using separate pricing levels for light, moderate, and heavy pet hair. Light removal might involve extra vacuuming and brushing. Moderate removal could require repeated passes with specialized tools. Heavy removal might involve working through several areas of carpet and upholstery individually.

The important thing is to connect the additional charge to the actual work involved. Do not promise complete removal in every situation. Some fabrics and carpet materials hold hair that is extremely difficult to remove completely.

Calculate What the Extra Work Actually Costs

Before deciding what to charge, estimate the additional labor and supplies.

For example, suppose a vehicle needs an extra 1.5 hours of pet hair removal. If your internal labor cost is $25 per hour, that adds $37.50 in labor.

Now suppose you use $7 in additional supplies and allocate $5 for equipment wear. Your additional job cost is $49.50.

If you charge the customer an additional $85, you have $35.50 remaining after those estimated costs.

That is a much better way to evaluate an add-on than picking a price because another detailer advertises the same service for that amount.

Remember that your regular package still has its own labor and expenses. The pet hair charge should cover the additional work, not replace the price of the interior detail.

Explain the Charge Before Starting and Track the Time on Every Job

Customers are more likely to accept an extra charge when they understand why it applies. Explain that your standard interior package includes ordinary vacuuming, but heavily embedded pet hair requires additional labor and specialized tools.

Put the pet hair removal charge on its own line in the quote. That way, the customer can see the difference between the standard detail and the extra service.

If the vehicle is significantly worse than the photos suggested, contact the customer before doing additional work. Explain what you found and get approval for the revised price.

After completing the vehicle, record how long the additional work actually took. Compare that with your estimate and note which areas required the most effort.

Over time, you will build a better understanding of how much labor different conditions require. You can adjust your pricing levels based on real jobs instead of guessing.

Pet hair removal can be a worthwhile add-on, but only when you charge for the extra time it takes. Make sure the price covers the work, the supplies, and the equipment you use.
