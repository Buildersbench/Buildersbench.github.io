How to Price a Move-Out Cleaning Job Without Losing Money

November 19, 2026

Move-out cleaning can be a good job for a cleaning business, but it is easy to underestimate the work. An empty house still needs cabinets, appliances, baseboards, bathrooms, and floors cleaned, and the customer may expect everything to be ready for a final inspection. If you quote it like a regular maintenance clean, you can end up doing several hours of extra work for the same price. Here is how to build a move-out cleaning quote that covers the actual job.

Understand what makes a move-out clean different

A regular cleaning visit usually focuses on keeping an already maintained home clean. A move-out clean starts with a different expectation. Customers often want the inside of cabinets, drawers, closets, appliances, and other areas that are skipped during routine visits.

Before giving a price, find out whether the property is completely empty, whether appliances need cleaning inside, and whether the customer expects interior windows or garage cleaning.

Ask for photos or arrange a walkthrough if you cannot judge the condition from a conversation. An empty property with light dirt is a different job from one with greasy kitchen surfaces, stained bathrooms, and neglected floors.

Calculate the labor, supplies, travel, and overhead

Start by estimating how many hours the job will take. If you send more than one cleaner, calculate total labor hours rather than just the time spent at the property.

For example, two cleaners working three hours each use six labor hours. At an actual labor cost of 25 dollars per hour, that is 150 dollars in labor.

Do not forget setup, loading supplies, travel, and cleanup. If your employees are paid for travel time, include it. If you work alone, your own time still has a cost. A job is not profitable just because you did not write yourself a paycheck for the hours you worked.

Move-out cleaning can use more supplies than a normal visit. Degreaser, glass cleaner, floor products, scrub pads, and other consumables all add to the cost.

Travel matters too, especially when the property is outside your usual service area. Include fuel and vehicle expenses rather than treating the drive as free.

You also need to cover the costs that keep your business running. Insurance, phone service, equipment maintenance, and advertising do not disappear when you are working at a customer's property.

A worked example for a move-out clean

Suppose the job requires two cleaners working three hours each. Labor costs 150 dollars, supplies cost 18 dollars, travel costs 22 dollars, and allocated overhead costs 30 dollars.

Your total job cost is 220 dollars.

If you want a 25 percent profit margin, divide 220 by 0.75. Your required selling price is 293.33 dollars. You could quote 295 dollars.

That price is based on the work you expect to perform, not what another cleaning company happens to charge. If the actual property condition is worse than the customer described, stop and get approval for additional work before continuing.

Put the scope in writing

A move-out quote should make it clear what is included. Separate the standard cleaning from optional services such as inside the oven, inside the refrigerator, garage cleaning, or interior windows.

Also explain what is excluded. Full wall washing, carpet restoration, heavy trash removal, and repairs should not quietly become part of the original price.

State your payment terms and cancellation policy. If the customer needs the property ready for an inspection, agree on the completion date before accepting the job.

A written quote protects both sides. The customer knows what they are paying for, and you have something to refer to if the requested work changes.
