How much should a handyman charge per hour?

November 16, 2026

Most handymen set their hourly rate by asking around: the other guy charges $45, so I charge $40 to win the job. That is how you end up busy all week and broke at the end of the month. Your rate has to come from your own costs, not from someone else's.

The three numbers behind any rate

Your wage. What you would pay someone else to do the work — not zero, and not whatever is left over after the bills. If you would hire a decent hand at $25 an hour, your own hour is worth at least that.

Your overhead per billable hour. Fuel, insurance, your phone, tool wear, the van. Add up a month of it and divide by the hours you actually bill in a month — not the hours you work, the hours a customer pays for. Quoting, driving, and paperwork are real hours, and nobody bills for them directly, so the billable hours have to carry them.

Your margin. Profit is not what is left over. It is a line in the price, on purpose — 15 to 20 percent on top of wage plus overhead is a reasonable place to start. Without it, one slow week or one broken tool eats the whole month.

A worked example

Say your wage is $25 an hour. Your overhead runs $1,200 a month, and you bill about 100 hours a month. That is $12 of overhead on every billable hour, so your cost is $37 an hour before you have made a cent. Add a 20 percent margin and the rate is about $44 an hour.

Now change one number: if you only bill 80 hours a month, the overhead share is $15, the cost is $40, and the same margin needs about $48 an hour. Fewer billable hours means a higher rate — which is exactly why "I charge less because I am cheaper" usually means "I have not done the math."

Why the other guy's rate tells you nothing

His overhead is not yours. He might work out of a paid-off truck with no insurance and a spouse covering the phone bill, or he might carry a crew, a shop, and a loan. A rate that keeps him fed can starve you, and a rate that keeps you fed can look expensive next to his. The only rate that matters is the one your own numbers produce.

Quote the job, not the hour

Once you know your hourly cost, stop selling hours. A customer does not want to buy four hours of you — they want a fixed faucet, a patched wall, a door that closes. Quote the job at a flat price built from your hourly cost plus materials, and the rate conversation mostly goes away. The customer gets certainty; you get paid for the value, not the clock.
