How to Price Fall Cleanup Jobs Without Losing Money on Leaves and Debris

November 20, 2026

Fall cleanup can be good seasonal work for a landscaping business, but it is also easy to underestimate. A yard that looks like a quick leaf removal can turn into several hours of blowing, raking, bagging, hauling, and disposal. If you quote based on how the property looks from the driveway, you can end up spending more time and money than the job pays for.

Look at the Work Before Giving a Price

Start by walking the property instead of estimating from the lawn size alone. Look at how much leaf coverage there is, whether leaves are wet or packed into landscaping beds, and how much material needs to be removed.

Pay attention to trees, fences, patios, driveways, and other areas where leaves collect. A yard with a large lawn and only a light covering might take less time than a smaller property with several trees and heavily covered beds.

Also find out what the customer expects. Some customers want leaves blown off the lawn and into wooded areas. Others want everything collected, bagged, and hauled away. Those are different jobs and should not receive the same quote.

Estimate Labor Based on the Actual Tasks

Break the cleanup into the work you will actually perform. Include blowing leaves out of beds, raking corners, clearing walkways, collecting debris, loading the truck, and cleaning up the property before leaving.

For example, suppose you estimate that a property will take 3 hours to complete. If you assign a labor cost of $30 per hour, your labor cost is $90.

That estimate should include your own working time if you are doing the job yourself. Your time is not free just because you are not writing yourself a paycheck for every hour.

If you expect to make multiple trips or need another worker, include that labor too. Do not quote a job based on the fastest possible completion time. Use a realistic estimate that allows you to finish the property properly.

Do Not Forget Disposal and Equipment Costs

Leaves and debris have to go somewhere. If the customer expects everything hauled away, include the cost of disposal and the time required to transport it.

Suppose your example job has a $35 disposal charge, $15 in bags and supplies, and $10 in fuel. Combined with the $90 labor cost, your direct job cost is $150.

You should also account for equipment wear, maintenance, insurance, and other business overhead. A cleanup that requires heavy equipment use or several truck loads should not be priced like a simple blow-off.

Make sure you know whether the customer is providing bags or whether you are supplying them. Clarify whether branches, sticks, and other yard debris are included or charged separately.

Build a Profit Margin Into Every Cleanup Quote

Once you know the costs, decide what you need to charge to make the job worthwhile.

Using the example above, suppose you allocate another $20 for business overhead. Your estimated total cost is now $170.

If you quote the customer $225, you have $55 remaining after those estimated costs. That is the amount available as profit, assuming the job stays within the estimated scope.

Do not treat the money left after paying for disposal as profit. Your labor, fuel, supplies, and equipment all have to be covered first.

Keep your estimates consistent from one property to the next. Record the actual time and expenses after each cleanup, then compare them with what you originally estimated. That is how you learn which properties take longer than expected and improve your pricing for the next fall season.
