How to Quote a Handyman Job So Materials Do Not Eat the Profit

November 18, 2026

A handyman quotes 300 dollars for a repair, buys 90 dollars of parts, and figures he made 210. Then the second trip, the extra hour, and the gas eat into it, and the job pays far less than he thought. Materials are where handyman jobs quietly lose money: the quote prices the part and forgets everything around it.

The part is not the whole materials cost

The price tag on the part is only the start. There is the drive to the supply house, the time finding the right fitting, and the second trip when the first one does not fit. And the part you buy and never use still costs you.

If you charge only for the part, you pay for all of that yourself. Your quote needs a line for the part and a line for getting it, or a markup on materials that covers both. Either way, the cost has to show up somewhere, or it comes out of your pay.

A worked faucet swap

Say a customer wants a bathroom faucet replaced. The faucet costs 85 dollars. The swap takes 1.5 hours on site at 40 dollars an hour, so labor is 60. The supply house run takes 45 minutes, 30 dollars of your time, plus 8 dollars of gas. Your share of overhead is 12.

Add it up: 85 plus 60 plus 30 plus 8 plus 12 comes to 195 dollars. That is your cost before any profit. If you want a 20 percent margin, divide 195 by 0.80 and you get 243.75 dollars, so quote 245.

Quote it at 200 instead, the way many handymen would, and you keep 5 dollars on a job that took more than two hours of your day. The job did not lose money, but it paid you almost nothing.

Mark up the parts or charge for the run

There are two honest ways to cover it: a separate supply run charge on the quote, say 25 to 40 dollars, or a markup on materials of 15 to 20 percent, which covers the trips, the wrong parts, and the returns.

On that 85 dollar faucet, a 20 percent markup adds 17 dollars, bringing materials to 102. It is not padding. It is the price of the fittings that did not fit and the gas to fetch the third. Pick one method and use it on every job.

Whatever you choose, say it on the quote. Customers accept a supply run charge they can see. They argue about a price that looks high with no explanation.

Write the materials down and check them after

After the job, write down what the parts really cost and how long the runs really took. If the faucet swap keeps coming in over your quote, your markup is too low or your labor time is too short.

Keep the quote and the real costs side by side for a few weeks. The jobs that lose money will show themselves fast.
